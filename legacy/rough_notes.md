The business question, restated simply: When a seller discounts a product more heavily, do customers actually engage with it more and rate it better — or does it just look that way because heavy discounts happen to cluster in categories that were already going to do well?

That second possibility — a confound — is the whole reason this phase exists. If you skip straight to "discount correlates with rating, done," you might be reporting a coincidence of category mix rather than a real effect. A real analyst's job is to rule that out before making a recommendation, because if it is just category mix, telling a business "discount more to boost ratings" would be actively bad advice.

Walking through what each step buys you, in order:

Log-transform the count variables. rating_count ranges from 2 to 426,973 — a few blockbuster products would otherwise dominate every statistic and hide what's happening for typical products. This just puts everything on a comparable scale.
Raw correlation (Pearson + Spearman). This is the naive, surface-level answer — "on its face, does more discount go with higher rating/engagement?" This is the number a rushed analysis would stop at. We compute it, but we don't trust it yet.
ANOVA — proving the confound is real, not hypothetical. Before controlling for category, we check: does discount depth actually differ by category? If yes (and it will), that's concrete evidence — not just a theoretical worry — that category could be distorting step 2's raw number. This step is what makes the next step justified rather than just "something analysts always do."
The controlled regression — this is the actual answer to the business question. We re-run the discount-vs-rating relationship, but this time holding category and price tier constant. Whatever's left over in the discount coefficient is the part of the relationship that discount depth itself explains, independent of what's being discounted. This is the number that would actually go in a recommendation to a business: does discounting work on its own merits, or was the apparent effect just "expensive/popular categories happen to discount less and rate better anyway"?
Robust standard errors. A technical correction, not a new question — it just makes sure the significance (p-value) on that controlled coefficient is trustworthy given that this kind of data doesn't behave the way simple regression assumes.
Writing the finding down. Translating "coefficient = X, p = Y" into a sentence a hiring manager or business stakeholder could actually act on.

1. The Relationship Detector: r (Correlation Coefficient)Think of r as a tug-of-war rope between two variables (e.g., Discount and Rating). It ranges from -1 to +1. When r increases (moves toward +1): It means a strong positive relationship. As one variable goes up, the other goes up. (Example: Height goes up, weight goes up). When r decreases (moves toward -1): It means a strong negative relationship. As one variable goes up, the other goes down. (Example: Discount goes up, rating goes down). When r moves toward 0: The rope goes slack. There is no relationship; the two variables don't care about each other. 2. The Confirmatory Judge: p-value (Probability)Think of the p-value as the "Fluke Detector" or a lie detector test. It measures the probability that your result is just a random coincidence. When p is tiny (Decreases below 0.05): SUCCESS! The result is statistically significant. It means the pattern is real, highly trustworthy, and definitely not a random fluke. When p increases (Above 0.05): DANGER! The result is insignificant. The pattern you think you see is likely just random noise or a coincidence. 💡 Easy way to remember: "If p is low, the status quo must go (the result is real). If p is high, it's a random guy (ignore it)." 3. The Group Difference Maker: F-statistic (ANOVA)Think of F as a megaphone comparing groups (e.g., comparing discount averages across categories). It measures if the differences between group averages are louder than the messy noise inside the groups. When F increases (Grows larger): The megaphone gets louder. It means the groups are wildly different from each other. (Example: Electronics and Office Products have completely different corporate discounting strategies). When F decreases (Moves toward 0 or 1): The megaphone gets quiet. It means the groups are basically identical, and any small differences are just minor background noise. 4. The Isolated Impact: Coef (Regression Coefficient)Think of the Coefficient as a knob in a controlled science lab. Because regression freezes other variables in place, the coefficient tells you exactly what happens when you turn only one knob. When a positive Coefficient increases: Turning the knob up makes the outcome go up even faster.When a negative Coefficient decreases (becomes more negative, e.g., -0.22 \rightarrow -0.26): The negative impact is getting stronger. Turning the knob up drags the outcome down even harder.When it moves toward 0: The knob is broken.
